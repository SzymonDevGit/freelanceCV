AI hallucination rates went up, not down

By Szymon Pecherski 2 August 2026 7 min read

Everyone assumes models get more reliable with each release. I pulled two snapshots of Vectara's hallucination leaderboard — 52 models from 2024, 105 from 2026 — and the average got noticeably worse. More usefully, something changed in how models fail.

The short version

On Vectara's summarisation benchmark, the mean hallucination rate across tested models rose from 6.25% in 2024 to 10.24% in 2026, and the median from 5.10% to 9.60%. The share of models hallucinating on under 5% of summaries collapsed from 44% to 9%. A new pattern also appeared: in 2026 longer summaries hallucinate more (13.0% at 125+ words versus 8.1% under 75 words), a relationship that did not exist in 2024.

What is actually being measured

This matters more than the numbers, so it goes first. Vectara's leaderboard does not measure "how often an AI lies". It measures one narrow, well-defined thing: given a source document and an instruction to summarise using only facts from that document, how often does the model introduce something the document does not support. The judge is Vectara's own HHEM-2.3 model, run over 7,700+ articles that are deliberately kept private so nobody can train against them.

That design has real consequences for how far you can stretch the result:

  • It is summarisation faithfulness, not general factual accuracy. A model that invents things when asked open questions could still score well here.
  • Vectara notes it does not capture every way a model can hallucinate, and calls the benchmark "a good start" rather than definitive.
  • Models that refuse to answer, or return very short responses, are filtered out — so the answer rate column matters alongside the headline number.
  • The judge is itself a model. It has its own error rate.

So: a useful, consistent yardstick for one important behaviour. Not a verdict on which AI is "best". Everything below should be read inside those walls.

The average model got worse

The 2024 snapshot covers 52 models; the 2026 snapshot covers 105. Mean hallucination rate rose from 6.25% to 10.24% and the median from 5.10% to 9.60% — so this is not one outlier dragging an average around, the whole distribution moved.

The obvious objection is sample composition: the 2026 board has twice as many models and includes a lot of small open-weight ones that would drag any average down. That is a fair challenge, so here is the same question asked of flagships only, where the comparison is like for like.

The pattern holds. On this specific task, today's flagship models are less faithful to the source document than their two-year-old equivalents. Meanwhile the best score on the board improved slightly, from 2.50% to 1.80%. The ceiling rose; the floor fell out.

Worth being precise about what this does and does not say. Newer models are better at plenty of things — reasoning, code, instruction following, tool use. Summarising a document without embellishing it is simply not one of the things that improved, and it happens to be the behaviour most business AI workflows depend on.

The finding that changes how you build

This is the part I did not expect. I bucketed every model by its average summary length and compared the hallucination rate in each bucket.

In 2024, how long a model's summaries ran told you nothing about whether it would make things up. In 2026 it tells you a lot: models averaging 125+ words hallucinate at 13.0%, against 8.1% for those staying under 75 words. That is a 60% higher rate for the verbose group.

The mechanism is visible in the model names. The longest-output models on the 2026 board are overwhelmingly the reasoning-tuned ones, and they cluster at the bad end: o3-pro at 23.3%, Grok 4 Fast Reasoning at 20.2%, GPT-5.1-high at 12.1% while averaging 254 words per summary — three times the length of GPT-4 Turbo's summaries in 2024. Models trained to elaborate, elaborate. When the task is "repeat only what this document says", elaboration is failure.

It is a correlation, not a law. The single most faithful model in the 2026 set, antgroup/finix_s1_32b at 1.8%, averages 172 words — long and accurate. Length is a risk factor, not a verdict.

What I'd actually do with this

Four things, if you are putting a model anywhere near a document your business relies on:

  1. Constrain the output length. The cheapest reliability win available. If you ask for a summary, cap it — "no more than 60 words" is a guardrail, not a style preference.
  2. Do not assume the newest model is the safest. For extraction and summarisation, benchmark the model you are replacing before you replace it. On this task, several two-year-old models beat their own successors.
  3. Do not use a reasoning model for faithful extraction. They are built to add reasoning. That is exactly the wrong instinct when the requirement is "add nothing".
  4. Keep a human in the loop where the output is load-bearing. At a 10% average rate, one in ten summaries contains something the source did not say. If that feeds a decision, a report or a client, it needs checking.

About that "$100 billion" number

The figure doing the rounds is $67.4bn of global business losses to AI hallucinations in 2024. Scale it by the rise in the mean rate — 10.24 ÷ 6.25 = 1.64 — and you land somewhere north of $110bn for 2026, which makes a very shareable headline.

I am not going to stand behind that number, and here's why

I could not trace the $67.4bn to a primary source. It is widely repeated across AI statistics round-ups, each citing the last. Until someone shows me the methodology, it is a number of unknown origin, and multiplying an unverifiable figure by a real one produces an unverifiable figure with a false air of precision.

The scaling is also wrong on its own terms: it assumes losses move with the hallucination rate alone, while AI adoption has grown enormously over the same period. If anything that makes the true figure larger — but "larger than an unverifiable number" is not analysis.

The rate change is real, measured, and reproducible from the data below. That is the part worth quoting.

Method and data

Two snapshots of the Vectara leaderboard, one from 2024 and one from 2026, tidied into a single CSV: model name, hallucination rate, factual consistency rate, answer rate and average summary length. Every figure in this post is computed from that file — the charts are generated from it directly, so they cannot drift from the numbers.

Length buckets are non-overlapping (<75, 75–100, 100–125, 125+ words) and I have shown the sample size in each, because the 2024 tail is thin. The correlation is Spearman's rank rather than Pearson, since the relationship is monotonic rather than linear and there are outliers at both ends.
