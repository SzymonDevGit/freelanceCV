85% of UK data job ads don’t tell you the salary

By Szymon Pecherski 10 August 2026 8 min read

Everyone who has applied for a job in the last few years suspects this. I scraped 9,559 UK data, analyst and adjacent postings from LinkedIn, cleaned them, and put a number on it. The number is worse than I expected — and where the salary is hidden turns out to be more interesting than the fact that it is.

The short version

Of 9,559 UK postings across 3,891 employers, only 1,429 (14.9%) state a salary. Remote roles are more than twice as likely to tell you as roles that say nothing about working pattern (24.9% versus 12.0%). London is the largest market by a distance and one of the least transparent, disclosing in 12.7% of adverts against 21.1% in Sheffield. Among the minority that do publish a figure, the median advertised midpoint is £54,000, and 7.8% of "ranges" are the same number twice.

What is in the data, and what is not

This goes first because it bounds everything below. The dataset is a snapshot of 9,559 unique live postings collected from LinkedIn in August 2026, split into two groups by search term: 4,008 explicitly analyst-titled roles and 5,551 adjacent ones — data engineering, finance, product, accounting and similar. Every row has a job id, title, employer, location, salary field and full description. There are 3,891 distinct employers.

What that design means for the conclusions:

  • It measures what employers publish on one platform, not what they pay. A company that withholds the salary in the advert and tells you at first interview is counted as withholding, because for the purpose of deciding whether to apply, it is.
  • The pay figures come only from the 14.9% that disclose. That is not a random sample of the market. Public bodies and organisations with banded pay structures publish more readily, so the medians below are best read as "typical of employers willing to publish", not "typical of the market".
  • Roles were captured once. Nothing here tracks how long a vacancy stays open, and 214 rows are the same title at the same employer in the same location — repostings, or genuinely parallel openings. They are flagged rather than removed, because there is no way to tell which from the data alone.

The headline: five in six adverts stay quiet

1,429 postings state a usable sterling salary. 8,130 do not. That is 85.1% of a market advertising professional roles at a median of £54,000 without saying so.

The interesting part is the variation. If withholding pay were simply how recruitment works, the rate would be flat across cities. It is not.

Belfast is the outlier at 5.8%, and Glasgow is not far behind at 8.1%. At the other end, Sheffield discloses in 21.1% of postings and Liverpool 19.8%. The gap between the best and worst is nearly four-fold, in the same country, in the same month, for broadly the same kinds of role.

London deserves its own sentence. It is the biggest data job market in the UK by a wide margin and it discloses less often than the national average. Whatever is driving disclosure, market size and competition for candidates is not it.

Remote roles are twice as likely to tell you

The strongest single predictor of disclosure in this dataset is not geography or seniority. It is working pattern.

A remote advert is more than twice as likely to carry a salary as one that says nothing about location arrangements. The plausible reason is competition: a remote role competes against every other remote role in the country rather than every role within commuting distance, and a candidate comparing twenty adverts will skip the ones that make them ask.

Worth noting the direction of that inference is not established by this data. Employers who are already open about pay may simply be the same employers who are open about flexibility. Both are symptoms of how an organisation writes adverts.

The counts on the left are worth as much as the money on the right. 191 postings — 2.0% of the market — are explicitly junior, graduate or trainee. There are 6.2 openly senior roles for every one of them, and 14.9 once you count every level above mid. Whatever "get your foot in the door" means, it is not being advertised as such.

The honest caveat is the 68.1% that state no level at all. Plenty of those would take someone early in their career; they simply do not say so, which leaves a candidate guessing at the level as well as the salary.

The ladder behaves as you would expect, and senior individual contributors and managers land within a few hundred pounds of each other. Two rows carry health warnings: 26 junior postings and 10 director postings are small enough that a handful of unusual adverts move the median, so treat those as indicative only.

Corrected 10 August 2026. This table first published with lead/manager at £56,025, below the senior figure — an apparently interesting result that was entirely an artefact of my own title parser. Matching the word "management" swept 311 Management Accountant and Management Consultant titles into the manager bucket. Matching "manager" instead puts lead/manager at £63,075 and the anomaly disappears.

Location adds about a fifth. London postings that publish pay have a median midpoint of £62,500 against £52,500 everywhere else — a £10,000 premium, or 19%. Whether that survives London housing costs is a different article.

One in thirteen "ranges" is a single number

Every salary on LinkedIn is stored as a range, which makes an unusual check possible: how many of those ranges are actually a range?

111 of the 1,429 — 7.8% — have an identical floor and ceiling. £42,000 to £42,000. That is a fixed salary presented in a field designed to imply negotiating room. Of the rest, the median range is £9,216 wide, or 14.3% of the midpoint, which is a genuine band rather than a gesture.

This is a small finding and I like it a lot, because it is the kind of thing you only see if you look at the shape of the data rather than the summary statistics. A mean salary would have hidden it completely.

The most-requested tool pays the least

Counting tool mentions across all 9,559 descriptions produces the least surprising ranking in data work, and then one genuinely awkward result.

Excel is named in 32.1% of all postings — more than SQL (24.8%), more than Power BI (18.2%), nearly twice Python (17.3%). Anyone who has been told spreadsheets are finished should sit with that number. Among analyst-titled roles specifically it is higher still, at 35.2%.

And the median advertised salary for postings naming Excel is £51,198 — the lowest of every tool with enough salaried postings to measure, and about £2,800 below the overall median. Python postings sit at £60,500 and machine learning at £67,500.

This is a correlation and it is heavily confounded. Excel appears in finance, operations and administrative roles that sit lower on the pay scale; machine learning appears in specialist roles that sit higher. Nobody gets a £9,000 rise by learning Python, and this data cannot tell you otherwise. What it does tell you is where the volume is against where the money is, and those are two different maps.

What I would actually do with this

If you are hiring. Publishing a salary is close to free and it is differentiating precisely because five in six of your competitors do not. You are currently filtering your applicant pool by tolerance for ambiguity, which is not a quality you were trying to select for. If you must publish a range, publish a real one — a floor and ceiling that are the same number is worse than no range, because candidates notice.

If you are job hunting. Assume the 85% is normal rather than a red flag; there is not much signal in it on its own. There is signal in the shape of what is published: a £9,000-wide band is a real band, and an identical floor and ceiling means the number is the number. Use the medians here as a sanity check, remembering they come from employers willing to publish.

If you are building a skills plan. Excel is not optional — a third of the market asks for it by name. But the frequency ranking and the pay ranking are close to inverted, so optimising purely for what appears most often walks you towards the lower-paid end of the market.

Method

The postings were collected with a Python scraper, then cleaned in pandas. The steps that changed the numbers, in order:

  • Deduplication. No duplicate job ids or URLs were present. 214 rows share a title, employer and location; flagged, not removed.
  • Salary parsing. Every value is a range string such as "£40,000.00/yr - £45,000.00/yr". Annual, hourly, daily and monthly units all appear; hourly is annualised at 1,950 hours and daily at 240 days, which is an assumption and affects 33 postings. Nine dollar and two euro postings are excluded from money figures but kept in the counts. 13 values annualising below £12,000 or above £400,000 are treated as unit errors and excluded.
  • HTML stripping. Descriptions arrive as markup. Matching tool names against the raw HTML miscounts — "R&D tax credit" reads as the R language, which inflated R from a true 4.2% to 5.6% before the fix.
  • Location. "London Area, United Kingdom" and "London, England, United Kingdom" are the same place; normalising to the leading city name cut 768 raw location strings to 758 and merged the London variants. Country- and region-only values are recorded as unspecified.
  • Seniority and working pattern are inferred from the title and description text. Seniority matches "manager" rather than "management", so that Management Accountants are not counted as managers — see the correction above. "Mentions hybrid" is not the same as "is hybrid", and "says nothing" is not the same as "office based".
